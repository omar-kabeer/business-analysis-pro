# Planning Stakeholder Engagement

## Finding everyone

BABOK 3.2 starts with identification, and identification is where the expensive misses happen. Work outward from the change rather than from the org chart.

Ask, for the process being changed: who performs it, who supervises it, who receives its output, who supplies its input, who audits it, who supports it when it breaks, who funds it, who is constrained by policy from allowing it, and who is affected but has no voice in the initiative. That last group, usually operational staff and end customers, is the one whose omission surfaces at acceptance.

Add the parties who can stop the work regardless of enthusiasm: security, data protection, procurement, legal, architecture, and any external regulator or counterparty. They are rarely on the sponsor's list and are frequently on the critical path.

## Analysing them

Five attributes, and the last two are the ones usually skipped.

| Attribute | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority | What can this person decide, alone, without asking | Whether they belong in a decision or only in a consultation |
| Interest | How much does the outcome affect them | How much of their attention you can reasonably ask for |
| Influence | Who do they change the mind of, formally or not | Sequencing. Some conversations must happen before others |
| Attitude | Supportive, neutral, sceptical, opposed, and why | The content of the engagement, not just its frequency |
| Availability | Realistically, how much time will they give | Whether the plan is achievable at all |

Availability is the constraint that quietly invalidates engagement plans. A plan requiring twelve hours a week from a head of operations who has two is not a plan. Establish the number, then design within it or escalate the gap before starting.

Attitude deserves the "and why". Opposition grounded in a previous failed programme needs different handling from opposition grounded in a real conflict of interest, and treating both as resistance to be managed is how analysts lose credibility.

## Designing the engagement

Plan per group, not per person, or the plan becomes unmaintainable and is abandoned within a month. For each group state four things.

**What they decide or contribute.** If the answer is nothing, they are being informed, and the plan should say so honestly rather than implying a consultation that will not happen.

**Cadence.** How often, and triggered by what. Fixed cadence for continuous involvement; event-triggered for approvals and escalations.

**Medium.** Workshop, interview, written review, demonstration, or asynchronous comment. Match the medium to the decision: a contested scope decision does not get resolved by circulating a document, and a routine confirmation does not need a workshop.

**What good engagement looks like.** The observable signal that the group is genuinely involved rather than nominally listed. Attendance is not the signal; decisions made on time is.

## Collaboration and the failure to plan for disagreement

Most engagement plans assume cooperation. Plan explicitly for two situations that will happen.

**Stakeholders who disagree with each other.** Name the decision, name who arbitrates, and state the time within which arbitration happens. Unresolved conflict between two stakeholders of equal authority is not a communication problem and will not be solved by another workshop; it needs an escalation path designed before it is needed.

**Stakeholders who do not engage.** State what happens when a group misses its input window: whether the work proceeds on a stated assumption, whether it stops, and who is told. Silence treated as consent is a defensible position only if it was agreed in advance and written down.

## Keeping it alive

A stakeholder analysis produced at initiation and never revisited is one of the reliably obsolete artefacts in business analysis. People change roles, attitudes shift as the change becomes concrete, and new parties appear once the solution shape is known.

Tie the review to events rather than to the calendar: a change in scope, a change in solution direction, a reorganisation, a missed decision, or an escalation. Each of those is evidence the map has moved.

## Boundaries

This skill plans the engagement. It does not conduct the interviews, workshops, or surveys, which is `elicitation`, and it does not write the communications, which is `communication`. The stakeholder register and RACI templates it produces are executed by those skills.

Where the engagement plan reveals that no one holds the authority a decision needs, that is a governance defect rather than an engagement defect. Take it to `references/governance-and-information-planning.md`.
