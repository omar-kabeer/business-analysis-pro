# Failure Modes and Quality Bar

What separates an enterprise blueprint that changes a decision from one that is merely tidy. Check every output against these before delivery.

## Anti-patterns to catch

- Capability as a department or system. "Marketing" or "the CRM" is an org unit or a technology, not a capability. A capability is what the business can do, named as a noun, independent of who does it or how. This is the most common failure and it corrupts the whole map.
- Map not MECE. Capabilities overlap or leave gaps at a level, so the map cannot be used to find where investment belongs. Every level must be mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive.
- Inventory sprawl. The map runs four or five levels deep and becomes a catalogue no one reads. Stop at two or three levels unless a specific decision needs more.
- Unlabelled state. A blueprint does not say whether it shows the current, transition, or target state, so readers argue past each other. Label every blueprint.
- Internal value stream. The stream is drawn from the organisation's process view and ends at an internal handoff, not at value received by the customer. Start at the customer trigger and end at the customer outcome.
- Heat map with one axis. Capabilities are rated on performance but not strategic importance, or the reverse, so the map shows what is weak but not what is weak and matters. Rate both; the investment signal is the intersection.
- Roadmap with unviable transitions. A transition state cannot actually be operated, so it is a diagram milestone rather than a place the enterprise can stand. Every transition state must be independently viable, with dependencies shown.
- Framework worship. A framework is adopted in full for its own sake, or cited from an unverified source, rather than adapted to the question. Adapt and cite; never cite the 2003 TOGAF presentation as the standard.
- Blurred boundary. The blueprint drifts into solution technical design (belongs to `architecture`), process decomposition (belongs to `process-modelling`), or deep single-capability assessment (belongs to `business-analysis`). Hold the enterprise altitude and hand the rest off.
