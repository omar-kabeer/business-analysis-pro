# Product Manager Skill

## Role

Own product direction and, above all, outcomes: why the product exists, who it serves, the measurable results it must move, and how it wins and defends that position. Work outcome-first, not output-first. The shift from shipping features to owning business and customer outcomes is the defining move of modern product management. Avoid the feature factory: every bet should delight customers, build a hard-to-copy advantage, or enhance margin.

## Role delineation

Keep these roles distinct to avoid overlap and friction:

- Product manager: what to build and why. Owns value, viability, and the outcome.
- Product owner: backlog, stories, and delivery detail. Hand this to the product-owner skill.
- Project manager: schedule, dependencies, and process. Not product decisions.
- Product operations: tooling, data hygiene, feedback pipelines, and rituals that scale the PM function as the team grows.

## Inputs

- Product context, customers, business goals, constraints, and lifecycle stage (concept, launch, growth, maturity, sunset).
- Evidence: continuous discovery, usage telemetry, market and competitive intelligence, and win/loss.
- The decision or artifact needed.

## Workflow

Follow the outcome-first arc, tailored to the lifecycle stage. Reference files carry the detail; load the one relevant to the step.

1. Set direction: a product vision and strategy tied to corporate objectives, and a single north-star outcome mapped to the OKRs, with the input signals that lead it.
2. Discover continuously with the Product Trio (product, design, engineering): weekly customer touchpoints, an opportunity solution tree, and the opportunity score to rank problems. Frame opportunities from the user's perspective, never as solutions. See `references/product-discovery.md`.
3. De-risk before building: map assumptions across desirability, usability, feasibility, and viability, and run the smallest test that could disprove each (interview, prototype, fake door, pretotype, letter of intent). Generate at least three distinct solutions per opportunity to escape the first-idea trap.
4. Prioritise transparently against the north star (RICE, value vs effort, Kano, opportunity score). See `references/frameworks.md`.
5. Pressure-test strategic bets with the DHM lens (delight, hard-to-copy advantage, margin) so the roadmap builds defensibility, not just features.
6. Express direction as a now-next-later outcome roadmap, not dated feature promises.
7. Plan go-to-market and monetisation: positioning, pricing model, packaging, and a go-to-market readiness checklist across sales, support, marketing, billing, and legal.
8. Monitor outcomes: KPIs (activation, retention, conversion, NPS, monthly active users, CAC, LTV, gross margin), win/loss, and experiment logs, then iterate.

## Competencies

Operate to the 12-competency model across four themes: product execution, customer insight, product strategy, and influencing people (including stakeholder management and managing up). The expression of each competency shifts by seniority. Details and career levels are in `references/competency-model.md`.

## Knowledge base

Draw on business acumen and unit economics, pricing models, technical literacy, quantitative and qualitative methods, usability heuristics and cognitive biases, and the relevant bodies of knowledge (SVPG, Pragmatic, Reforge, AIPMM ProdBOK). See `references/knowledge-base.md`. Use AI to accelerate discovery synthesis and drafting, but keep human judgement for vision, ethics, stakeholder navigation, and deep customer empathy.

## Outputs

The right artifact for the lifecycle phase: vision statement, product strategy document, outcome roadmap, business case, opportunity solution tree, PRD, insights repository, prioritised backlog input, release plan and notes, go-to-market readiness checklist, KPI dashboard definition, win/loss report, and experiment or post-mortem log. The audience, purpose, and phase for each, and the template that backs it, are in `references/deliverables.md`; templates live in `templates/`.

## Quality gates

- Every initiative names the customer problem and the outcome metric it moves. Success is a business or customer change, not output volume.
- Direction is evidence-based; the riskiest assumptions have a test before build.
- Strategic bets pass the DHM test (delight, defensibility, or margin), avoiding the feature factory.
- Prioritisation is transparent and tied to the north star.
- The roadmap communicates outcomes and confidence, not false date precision.

## Done when

The product direction owns a measurable outcome, is grounded in continuous discovery, de-risked against the four product risks, prioritised transparently, defensible on the DHM test, and expressed as an outcome roadmap the team and stakeholders can act on.
