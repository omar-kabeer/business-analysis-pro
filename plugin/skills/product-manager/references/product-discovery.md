# Product Discovery and Strategy Reference

Grounded in modern product practice (Marty Cagan and SVPG, Teresa Torres, Melissa Perri, Roman Pichler).

## Outcome over output

Lead with problems and measurable results, not feature lists. Define a north-star metric that captures the value customers get, plus a small set of input signals that lead it. Modern product organisations evaluate PMs on outcomes (retention, expansion, activation, drop-off reduction), not on how many features shipped.

## The Product Trio and continuous discovery

Discovery is a team sport run continuously, not a phase. The Product Trio (product manager, product designer, and a tech lead or engineer) makes discovery decisions together and holds a customer touchpoint at least weekly. Small, frequent research beats big upfront research.

## Jobs to be Done

People hire a product to make progress in a situation. Capture the job, the circumstances, and the desired progress: when [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]. Use retrospective narrative interviewing (ask users to recount a specific recent instance of the pain) rather than asking what features they want.

## Opportunity Solution Tree

A visual, non-linear hierarchy linking outcomes to experiments:

- Desired outcome at the root: one quantitative metric mapped to the OKRs (for example raise 30-day retention from 28 percent to 42 percent).
- Opportunity space: customer problems, pains, and desires from continuous research, framed from the user's view, never as solutions.
- Solution space: feature concepts addressing a prioritised opportunity. Generate at least three distinct solutions per opportunity to avoid the first-idea trap.
- Experiment space: rapid, low-cost tests that validate assumptions before committing engineering.

Do not attach a solution that has no opportunity above it.

## Prioritising opportunities: the opportunity score

Rank opportunities by importance and satisfaction (Dan Olsen). Importance and satisfaction are rated by customers; the highest priority opportunities are high importance and low satisfaction. A common form is opportunity score equals importance plus the greater of zero or (importance minus satisfaction). Prioritise the underserved, important problems.

## De-risking: assumption mapping and the four risks

Break a proposed solution into its assumptions and test the riskiest first across the four product risks:

- Value (desirability): will customers want it?
- Usability: can they use it?
- Feasibility: can we build it?
- Viability: does it work for the business?

Test with the smallest thing that could disprove the assumption: interview, prototype, fake door, concierge, pretotype, or a skin-in-the-game test such as a landing-page pretotype or a letter-of-intent signup, before writing production code.

## Roadmap

Prefer now-next-later over dated Gantt promises. Communicate outcomes and confidence levels. Revisit as discovery changes the evidence.
