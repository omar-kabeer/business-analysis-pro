# Question Design and Bias

How to ask questions that draw out what people know rather than what the analyst expects, and how to spot the biases that distort elicitation. This reference supports Conduct Elicitation (4.2) and Confirm Elicitation Results (4.3) in BABOK, with the techniques Interviews (10.25), Workshops (10.50), and Survey or Questionnaire (10.45).

## Open, then narrow

Start broad and let the person describe the work in their own terms ("Walk me through the last time you did this"), then narrow to specifics ("How long did that step take?"). Opening narrow imposes the analyst's frame before the person has shown theirs.

## Questions to avoid

| Type | Example | Problem | Better |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Leading | "Wouldn't a portal help?" | Suggests the answer | "What would help most?" |
| Loaded | "Why is AP so slow?" | Assumes blame | "Where does the delay happen?" |
| Double-barrelled | "Is the tool fast and easy?" | Two questions, one answer | Ask each separately |
| Hypothetical only | "Would you use feature X?" | People predict poorly | "When did you last need X, and what did you do?" |

## Biases to watch

- Confirmation bias: noticing evidence that supports the expected answer. Counter it by asking what would disprove the hypothesis.
- Anchoring: the first number mentioned shapes every later estimate. Ask for the person's figure before offering one.
- Availability: vivid recent incidents feel typical. Ask how often, and check against data.
- Social desirability: people describe the procedure, not what they actually do. Observe where possible.
- Groupthink: in workshops, the senior voice settles the question. Use silent individual input before discussion.

## Past behaviour over opinion

Questions about specific past events ("Tell me about the last invoice you rejected") give more reliable answers than questions about opinions or the future. Record the answer as said, and label it fact or opinion when capturing it.

## Worked example

A draft interview question for budget holders read: "Would a mobile app that reminds you to approve invoices make approvals faster?" It is leading, hypothetical, and double-barrelled (reminders and mobile). It was replaced with two questions: "Tell me about the last invoice you approved: where were you, and how did you find out it was waiting?" and "What made it take as long as it did?" Four of six budget holders mentioned being on site without a laptop, which the original question would have presumed rather than revealed.
