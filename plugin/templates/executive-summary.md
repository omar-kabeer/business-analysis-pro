# Executive Summary

## Purpose

Give a senior audience (executive, board, investor, steering committee) everything they need to make a decision, on one page, without reading the full document. The summary leads with the answer and the ask, then supports it. It is written last, from a completed analysis, and can front a business case, a proposal, an analysis, or a recommendation.

## Structure

Fill the sections in order. Keep the whole summary to about one page. Every sentence should help the reader decide; cut anything that does not.

### Bottom line

State the recommendation and the specific ask in one or two sentences, up front. The reader should be able to stop here and know what you want them to approve.

### Situation

The relevant context in a few sentences: where things stand and why this is on the table now.

### Complication

What changed or what problem forces a decision. This is the reason the reader cannot simply carry on as before.

### Resolution

The recommendation and the case for it, grouped into a few clear points:

- Recommendation: the option you propose.
- Value: the quantified benefit (financial and non-financial), with the time frame.
- Options considered: the main alternatives and why they lost, in one line each.
- Risks: the principal risks and how they are managed.
- Cost and effort: the investment and the rough timeline.

### Decision required

State exactly what is being asked, of whom, and by when. Name the approvers and any conditions.

## Writing rules

- Lead with the answer, then the support (pyramid principle). The reader can stop at any level and still have a coherent message.
- Group the support into a few mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive points.
- Quantify value, cost, and risk wherever possible, and state confidence.
- Be honest about risk and assumptions; do not bury the downside.
- Keep figures consistent with the full document and its visuals.
- Cut hedging, jargon, and background that does not change the decision. Move depth to the full document or an appendix.

## Skeptical-reader test

For each claim, ask: how do we know, what if it is wrong, what would a board member challenge, and what does it cost to be wrong. Strengthen or remove anything that cannot survive that.

## Review criteria

- The recommendation and ask are clear within the first two sentences.
- The argument leads with the answer and is MECE beneath it.
- Value, cost, and risk are quantified and consistent with the source document.
- Length and tone fit a senior audience and nothing survives that does not aid the decision.
